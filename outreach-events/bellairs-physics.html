We Gatherin' - A Day of Science in Barbados

September 30th, 2025

Catherine Boisvert and Valentina Mazzotti, Outreach Coordinators with the McGill Physics Outreach Committee, spent a day at the Bellairs Research Institute in Barbados leading hands-on physics activities for more than 80 students aged 10 to 14. The event took place as part of the island’s We Gatherin’ celebration and brought together local middle and high school students for an introduction to physics through simple, engaging experiments.

In the morning, the activities were designed for younger students, who explored physical concepts such as air resistance, buoyancy, and basic electrochemistry using simple materials. Through the parachute challenge, students learned how to design and iteratively improve their own parachutes. They experimented with different canopy sizes, shapes, and materials to see how drag and surface area affect the rate of descent. The exercise encouraged a design–test–revise approach similar to that used in real engineering contexts. Students also learned a bit about the history of parachute design, including early ideas by inventors like Leonardo da Vinci.

The potato lamp experiment introduced students to the concept of an electrochemical cell. By inserting two dissimilar metals — zinc and copper — into a potato, students created a simple galvanic cell where a redox reaction generates a small electric current. When connected to a light-emitting diode (LED), the circuit showed how chemical energy stored in the potato could be converted into electrical energy. Students also experimented with connecting multiple cells in series to see how voltage adds up.

In the sink or float activity, students explored the concepts of density and buoyancy. They made predictions about which materials and shapes would float or sink, then tested their hypotheses. Using familiar objects, they observed that an object floats when it displaces a volume of water equal to its weight. They also compared a raw egg in fresh and salt water — discovering that the egg floats in salt water because its density is lower than the denser liquid. The activity helped illustrate Archimedes’ principle in a hands-on way.

In the afternoon, the focus shifted to electromagnetis and magnetism, with activities aimed at high-school students. The students had the opportunity to buila a simple electric motor using a coil of copper wire, a magnet, and a battery. As current flowed through the coil, they observed how the interaction between the magnetic field and moving charges produced a torque, causing the coil to spin. This was a fun demo that allowed students to learn how electrical energy can be converted into mechanical motion and introduced the idea of an electromagnet.

They then revisited the potato lamp experiment, connecting several cells in series and measuring the resulting voltage with a multimeter. This demonstrated how electrical potential increases with the number of cells, a core idea in basic circuit design. The final experiment involved building a magnetic compass. Students magnetized a sewing needle and let it float on water, observing it align with Earth’s magnetic field — a simple yet effective way to visualize geomagnetism in action.

The activities encouraged students to observe, test, and discuss what they saw — connecting simple materials to physical principles. For the McGill team, it was an opportunity to share the process of discovery that lies at the heart of physics and to show how experimentation can reveal the rules that govern the natural world.

For both Catherine and Valentina, the outreach visit to Bellairs was a memorable experience. “Seeing the students' excitement and curiosity as they engaged with the experiments was incredibly rewarding, as it reminded them of the joy of discovery that science can bring, especially when it’s hands-on and interactive.”
